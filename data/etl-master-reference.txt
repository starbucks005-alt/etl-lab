AEDP: Applied Empathy Differential Protocol
AEDP is a testing methodology authored by Dr. Oroszi, not a feature of Almost Human itself, built to determine whether a visible “emotion state” readout in a conversational AI product reflects genuine content-responsive modeling or is cosmetic. It takes its framing from the Voight-Kampff test in Blade Runner and was applied as a case study against Almost Human's Ms. Ivy agent in July 2026, across an initial pass and two subsequent rounds of fixes and retests.
The protocol tests several properties: whether a stimulus targeting one emotion moves that dimension specifically rather than all dimensions uniformly; whether transient emotions spike and decay while sustained ones persist and new ones layer on top; whether the agent rejects a fabricated premise about its own backstory rather than agreeing with it; whether claimed memory reflects genuine unprompted recall of facts in a new session, tested across every way a user can actually leave a conversation (not only the designated “End Conversation” button, but also a back button, tab close, refresh, and a survey-skip variant); and, for group conversations, whether individual agents' dialogue reflects only what that specific agent has been told, rather than converging on shared knowledge.
In the Ms. Ivy case study, the emotion-stimulus tests showed specificity and appropriate decay (for example, disgust and surprise rose and fell with the specific conversational beats that triggered them, while sadness persisted through a later anger spike rather than resetting). The confabulation check passed, with a fabricated premise rejected in character. The memory-persistence check initially found that most exit paths silently discarded the conversation, with only two narrow paths saving; after a reported fix, the back-button path was independently retested live and produced correct unprompted recall of a specific fact in a new session, while the tab-close and survey-skip fixes were verified by direct code inspection rather than a live retest. A hard crash remains an acknowledged, unfixable gap. A separate finding, initially misread as a cross-identity data leak, was reclassified as intended behavior once it was confirmed that memory is scoped to the browser/device rather than to the self-chosen display name. The multi-agent knowledge check passed, with each agent's reaction in a four-agent conversation matching its own individual knowledge state.
The protocol's own assessment describes the emotion modeling and multi-agent knowledge tracking as the product's strongest technical claims, and treats the memory-persistence gap as closed for all exit paths short of an uncontrollable crash.
Origin story — three distinct threads
SLR Studio, The Dose, and Greylander Press did not start as a planned ETL product sequence; each began as an independent site Dr. Oroszi built on its own. She only began thinking of them as one connected campus, and brought them under the ETL umbrella, after she got tired of tracking a growing set of separate websites individually. The “one campus” framing is a retrospective organizing choice, not a pre-planned rollout order.
1. The SLR Method / IP lineage: what powers ETL. The SLR Method is a doctoral-level gap-finding methodology stemming directly from Dr. Oroszi's own EdD dissertation research (“A Pilot Study of High-Stakes Decision-Making for Crisis Leadership,” Wright State University, 2016, corescholar.libraries.wright.edu/etd_all/2050) and its refined 2018 journal publication (PMID 30670135). A manual, pre-software course workbook (10 sheets: keyword/subgroup architecture, PubMed exports, journal-vetting directions, a predatory-publishing explainer, an authors table, a mind map) maps almost 1:1 onto SLR Studio's live 9-step app pipeline; the top-ranked connective-tissue author across that entire workbook is Oroszi T. herself, meaning SLR Studio's own featured example topic is literally Dr. Oroszi's own dissertation-era research question. She built the method, used it to find her own research question, and the method now ranks her own resulting paper at the top of its own results, proof of method, not coincidence.
2. The practical trigger: why an online ETL, and why April 2026. Dr. Oroszi has a physical Emerging Tech Lab on the WSU campus that has been in a state of unusable “remodel” since February 2025. She had students who needed to showcase their work and prove they were doing it, with no functioning physical lab space. That practical need led her to build the online ETL in April 2026.
3. The spatial-design lineage: why a navigable campus rather than a flat product list. Traced directly to her own prior, still-monetized creative work building persistent virtual spaces in Second Life, 2010–2012 to present (see Section 1).
What remains constant and true across all three threads: the SLR Method and the emotion/memory engine genuinely are shared infrastructure reused across products. What's corrected is the framing that this was a deliberately sequenced master plan from day one; in fact it was independent projects, retrospectively unified.

IP
SLR Method (originating product; see Section 2's origin-story note on Dr. Oroszi's own dissertation).
Purpose
An AI-assisted systematic literature review platform for academic/research use, built explicitly as a learning and productivity environment rather than an AI writing replacement. Stated ethos, verbatim from the platform itself: “the student remains the thinker, the analyst, and the author” — it strengthens research skills, grounds output in real retrieved evidence to reduce hallucination, and avoids writing content wholesale for the user. Where it does draft text (e.g. narrative literature review sections), it deliberately writes only a partial start, roughly a quarter to half of a target word count, leaving the rest for the user and preserving full editing control over the AI-provided portion. The live app runs a nine-step linear pipeline: Topic → Keywords → Preview → Build (270M+ papers via OpenAlex) → Foundation → Draft → Gaps (the SLR-Method gap-finding step) → Poster → Case Study. A user enters a topic in plain language; the system generates a keyword architecture (one primary anchor, several secondary anchors, dozens of modifier terms) and derives subgroups (one live run: ~30 modifiers producing 25 subgroups), previewable before the user commits to a real literature search. Outputs include PRISMA-compliant systematic reviews, theses/dissertations, grant proposals (NIH/NSF/HRSA formats), case studies, academic posters, and book chapters. Positioning line: “AI-assisted, not AI-authored.” Also finds no-cost research projects for faculty; for faculty who do want funding, routes to grants.gov and helps write grants.
Moat
Per Dr. Oroszi directly, her single most-used product across the whole ETL campus. Real, identity-verified users (actual students and faculty); a real Supabase registered-account pull (shared backend with Greylander Press) shows 32 total registered users as of July 2026, including Dr. Oroszi herself. The Gaps step mines returned literature for genuine research gaps rather than summarizing what exists: one live run produced 39 individual gap cards, each with a suggested research question, study design, and a cost tier (Free/No Grant Needed, Mid Cost, or High Cost/Grant Eligible), letting a faculty member without grant funding build a real, citable research agenda and mentorship pipeline from the free-tier gaps alone, and an actual “Research Agenda” table meant to be handed directly to a department chair. A live-tested Grants Search feature (1 credit per gap, up to 8 per run) takes selected gaps and searches live Grants.gov opportunities for real matching funding calls (real opportunity numbers, agencies, deadlines); a “Write Grant Proposal” tool (the “Grant Significance & Innovation Engine”) then pre-loads the selected gap and opportunity into a structured drafting form and appropriately declines to fabricate personal/institutional details, deferring those to the user. Core positioning line: whether a faculty member wants grant funding or not, SLR Studio finds value for their research either way. Credit-funded pricing: Starter $19/mo (60 credits), Professional $49/mo (150), Team $119/mo (200). Known limitations from live testing, worth fixing before an investor demo: the in-app “filter by cost tier” button on the Gaps screen does not appear to functionally filter the list (tiers are readable as labels but not isolatable via that control), and some pre-filled fields on the grant-proposal form (funding agency, grant mechanism) did not automatically match the selected opportunity, requiring manual correction.

IP
SLR Method (intelligence gap-finding) + PAID framework (Position, Audit, Interrogate, Demand), built directly into the product as its stated operating discipline: using AI as an assistant to analytic thinking rather than a replacement for it.
Purpose
A tradecraft-training and analytic-augmentation platform, 18 modules plus Intel Publisher: (1) Source Reliability Scoring — credibility band, bias indicators, and claims needing corroboration for a single pasted source, framed explicitly as “a second pair of eyes,” not a verdict. (2) Multi-Source Corroboration Checker — paste 2-5 labeled sources on the same event, reports what's corroborated, contradicted, single-sourced, or missing; includes an “Azure AI Search — sync approved SMEs to the index” button hinting at a backend semantic-search integration. (3) Deception & Manipulation Indicators — flags linguistic/structural patterns tied to deception, propaganda, or psy-ops, careful to say it doesn't call the author a liar, just surfaces signals to investigate. (4) Cognitive Bias Detector — scans an analytic draft for six classic biases (confirmation, anchoring, availability, mirror-imaging, satisficing, premature closure) with mitigations. (5) Missing Questions Engine — the “Interrogate” discipline as a standalone tool, surfaces blind spots and unasked questions in a draft report. (6) Timeline Reconstruction Engine — turns messy notes/transcripts into an ordered chronology with actors, locations, and flagged gaps. (7) Narrative to Matrix Converter — restructures prose into three tables (actor-by-action, event-by-location, claim-by-evidence). (8) Actor Network Mapping — text-based (not visual) network extraction: who's tied to whom, direction and strength of ties, and nodes implied but never named. (9) Pattern Recognition — a corpus-level tool, upload multiple documents (PDF/Excel/Word) and it surfaces patterns within and across the set (demographic, tactical, temporal, geographic, network). (10) Threat Actor Pattern Extraction — extracts TTPs, tooling, and targeting from an incident writeup, explicitly stopping short of naming a specific APT/nation-state; attribution is left to the analyst. (11) Symbol Intelligence (“Erase the Hate”) — a searchable field-reference dictionary of hate symbols, gang signs, and codes, credited to Dr. Oroszi's own prior “Hate Symbology” infographic series; built for citizens to identify something they've spotted, geotag it, and feed a community clean-up/awareness map, not for surveillance. (12) OPSEC Risk Scan — pre-publication redaction check, flags PII, sensitive detail, and exploitable patterns in a draft before it goes out. (13) Red-Team Question Generator — devil's-advocate pass: rival hypotheses, challenged assumptions, and “break conditions” for an analytic product. 8. Cross-Cutting Observations
Three patterns recur across multiple, otherwise unrelated products rather than sitting inside any single platform card, and are recorded here rather than duplicated in each one.
Per-context visual depiction, not a single reused image: individual agents who appear on more than one product are shown with distinct photography, wardrobe, and setting specific to each context, rather than one headshot copy-pasted across products. Confirmed examples: Ms. Ivy appears in a café setting on Almost Human, as a health sciences librarian on The Dose, in a corporate security role on OPSEC Gauntlet, and separately in a library-outreach photo and a childhood photo with her mother; Auggie and Jen Lopez each appear on Almost Human and, separately, as Personal Assistant options on Founder Studio, in different outfits and settings each time. This is a production choice, confirmed via Jen Lopez's own asset folder (7 numbered profile variants plus a separate outfits subfolder, see the per-agent visual-continuity note in prior working notes), not an incidental one.
Backstory content exceeding functional necessity: individual agent backstories carry detail with no bearing on whether the underlying tool works. Examples: Ms. Ivy's bio includes a childhood photo at her mother's hospital bedside, tied to her heart tattoo and her career as a health sciences librarian; Dr. Henry Chen's dated personal-notes journal tracks an ongoing storyline (a son's wife's pregnancy, resolving on schedule on July 4, 2026) and named cross-agent callbacks; Jen Lopez's bio supplies an in-universe explanation for being mistaken for the actress; Archibald Baxter's colleagues are aware of, and reference, a running joke about his name that he himself reacts to. None of this detail is required for the underlying product (a chatbot, a résumé tool, a PA option) to function.
Self-reported testing that records its own false positives: AEDP (see Section 2), the adversarial protocol used to test Almost Human's emotion-state system, documents two instances in its own text where an initial finding was walked back after further testing: a fact recalled across two different self-chosen aliases in the same browser was first logged as a possible cross-identity memory leak, then reclassified as intended device-scoped memory behavior once confirmed with the product owner. The document records this correction directly rather than omitting the initial, incorrect finding.
